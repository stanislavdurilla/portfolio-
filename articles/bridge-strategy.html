Foreign policy · Slovakia

Bratislava's bridge that no one asked to build

Robert Fico casts Slovakia as a bridge between Russia and the West. The strategy has failed before — and the reasons say more about domestic politics than foreign policy.

It's May 2025, and Robert Fico is the only EU leader present in Moscow at the commemoration of Russia's Victory Day, despite being warned by the EU's chief diplomat not to attend. A few months before that, Fico unexpectedly travelled to Moscow to meet with Putin directly. One recurring criticism of these visits is that Fico isn't always transparent with the public and the media about meetings with the Russian president. In the meantime, Fico pushes the narrative that there is a wall between Russia and the West, and he tries to build a bridge between the two.

It is a convenient metaphor for a small state like Slovakia. It implies relevance, diplomatic balance, and indispensability. Nevertheless, the bridge strategy has failed before. Slovakia already tried this in the mid-1990s, when Vladimír Mečiar's geo-economic bridge between Slovakia and Russia didn't result in any substantial gains or leverage. On the contrary, it resulted in Slovakia missing out on the first wave of NATO and EU enlargement, which put the country behind its regional Visegrad neighbours.

Foreign policy for domestic use only

What really should catch one's eye is that Fico's foreign policy is actually a domestic one in disguise. It is Fico's rhetoric that claims pragmatic neutrality combined with national sovereignty. This plays well with voters, for whom genuine, historically rooted affinity for Russia and real anxiety about inflation, energy, and grocery prices go hand in hand. Hate-and-fear rhetoric was central to his return to power in the September 2023 elections, and it has continued to structure Slovak politics since. The polarisation reached its most extreme point when, in May 2024, Fico survived an assassination attempt.

The bridge metaphor isn't for Brussels or Moscow. It is aimed at a domestic audience that wants him to look and act tough without actually leaving the EU and NATO – Slovakia joined both in 2004 and has not shown any serious intention to leave.

Economic interests talk

The clearest evidence that Slovakia's current foreign policy is positioning rather than strategy is its record on Ukraine's own accession. Despite confrontational rhetoric about Ukraine, its president, and the EU's Ukraine policy, Slovakia has generally supported Ukraine's long-term ambition to join the EU. This is not out of solidarity alone. Eastern Slovakia is the country's poorest and highest-unemployment region, which would benefit substantially from a border with another EU member state rather than a candidate at war. In this case, Slovakia's support for enlargement looks less like a principle and more like regional economic strategy dressed in geopolitical language.

The inconsistency is the tell. A genuine bridge state maintains a consistent position towards both ends of the bridge. Slovakia essentially runs two foreign policies at once. The loud one is aimed at Moscow and domestic audiences; the quieter one is aimed at Brussels and Slovakia's economic interests. It is the latter that is closer to the truth.

Why this isn't only about Slovakia

Slovakia is a useful case study for two reasons: the gap between rhetoric and interest is clearly visible, and Slovakia isn't a marginal player. It holds veto powers in both the EU and NATO, so the gap actually matters. Other small or mid-sized states can face similar pressures. Energy dependency, historical ties, or domestic polarisation are likely to produce their own version of the same gap. Watching how EU governments vote at the EU level is a more reliable signal, far more than what their leaders say in Moscow.
