Slovak chameleon in play: Pro-Russian at home, Pro-EU in Brussels

Institutional trust — not income or inflation — is what actually predicts Slovak support for the EU.

Slovakia is seen as a thorn in the EU's side, especially when it comes to Ukraine. Despite more than three years of war in Ukraine, Slovakia still heavily relies on Russian energy and resists pressure from Brussels to cut it out completely. Slovakia's prime minister frequently meets Russian President Vladimir Putin, and the country has seen some of the sharpest inflation in the bloc since the invasion began. According to every textbook on Euroscepticism, this should mean one thing and one thing only: eroding support for European integration. But the data says otherwise.

Using the 100.2 Eurobarometer survey from late 2023, I built a statistical model to predict whether Slovak citizens have a positive view of EU membership. The model correctly classified four out of five respondents, which represents a strong result for this kind of individual-level analysis. This should help to unsettle assumptions about how Slovak citizens feel about Brussels, despite the government's rhetoric.

Trust over the bills

As counter-intuitive as it may be, the single strongest predictor of pro-EU sentiment among Slovak citizens was not income, employment status or even the ability to pay bills – it was institutional trust. Respondents who trusted their national government, parliament and the EU as a whole were 11 times more likely to hold a positive image of the EU than those who didn't. Interestingly, financial hardship didn't play a significant role once the other factors were accounted for.

This marks a shift from how public opinion has conventionally been explained. For the past 30 years, it was believed that support for the EU was purely transactional. Essentially, people support the EU when it pays off, but once it stops paying off, the support diminishes quickly. That's what makes the results among Slovak citizens interesting. They challenge conventional theory, suggesting that Slovak support for the EU looks more like a loyalty test than a simple cost-benefit calculation. The question for Slovak citizens isn't "what has the EU done for me lately" but whether they can trust the people running the show, both in Bratislava and Brussels.

Ukraine second in line

The other significant driver of support for EU integration was the EU's policy towards Ukraine, namely its financial, humanitarian and military assistance. Respondents who agreed with the EU's response were more likely to support European integration in general, regardless of their own financial situation. In other words, Slovaks appear to evaluate the EU not as a subsidy machine but rather as a guarantor of security and stability.

These results matter because they reveal a genuinely sociotropic pattern – Slovak citizens weight collective outcomes instead of personal ones. This is what makes it counter-intuitive. Slovak politics is usually seen through the opposite lens: cheap gas, groceries, energy bills, and Robert Fico's insistence that Russian sanctions hurt ordinary Slovaks. The cost-of-living crisis is undoubtedly there; it just isn't what is moving the numbers the most.

What about Brussels

If institutional trust is the number one driver of public support, the EU's most effective investment in Slovak public opinion isn't another subsidy programme. It is credible governance, delivered through institutions Slovaks still believe in. Data shows that communication strategies built around economic benefits are unlikely to succeed. Instead, strategies highlighting that EU institutions are competent, transparent, and trustworthy carry far more weight with Slovak voters.

However, this doesn't mean that public opinion in Slovakia is fixed. Trust can erode as quickly as it was found to matter, particularly when democratic backsliding continues, and many political parties build their campaigns ahead of the 2027 election on Euroscepticism. But for now the story is far more interesting: Slovakia's relationship with Europe runs on confidence, not money, despite Fico's attempt to convince the world otherwise.
