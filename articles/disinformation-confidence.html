Confident but exposed: how Slovaks see disinformation

Slovaks see more disinformation than most Europeans and feel surer they can spot it, yet that confidence does not go with a different view of Russia.

It seems that never before has cohesion inside the EU been more important than today. The EU is dealing with the biggest war on its continent since WWII, now in its fifth year. The way the conflict ends will influence the EU's 450 million citizens for decades to come. The EU is at a crossroads, which is why it matters how its citizens experience disinformation. Using the latest Eurobarometer data, I look at how Slovaks see it and what that tells us.

The European Commission's spring 2026 Eurobarometer shows that Slovaks report coming across disinformation more often than the EU average: 84% compared with 73%. That is not surprising given the fact that Russia heavily focuses on Slovakia with its disinformation campaign, while Slovakia is one of the most vulnerable countries regarding disinformation (Turková et al., 2023). Additionally, 94% of Slovak citizens view disinformation as a major problem for democracy, compared with the EU average of 87%. This shows that Slovak citizens almost universally agree on this issue.

But there is a different finding that makes Slovakia's case interesting, and that is that 72% of Slovaks believe that they can easily identify disinformation, compared with the EU average of 59%. In a country where over two-thirds believe they can easily spot disinformation, 51% blamed the West or Ukraine for Russia's 2022 full-scale invasion, compared with 41% who blamed Russia (GLOBSEC, 2024). This gap between confidence and belief is what makes Slovakia's case worth a closer look.

My own analysis of the survey data shows that 50% of those confident in spotting disinformation hold a positive image of the EU, compared with 32% of those who are not. Yet the share holding a positive view of Russia is identical in both groups, at 27%, almost double the EU average of 14%. Feeling confident about spotting disinformation does not go hand in hand with a different view of Russia, which deserves closer attention.

The Eurobarometer data should be understood as people's own assessment of what disinformation is. What one person calls disinformation, another may see as legitimate opinion, and feeling able to spot it is not the same as actually spotting it. The relationships described here are correlations, not causes.

If confidence in spotting disinformation does not shift views of Russia, the answer may lie less in telling people what is false and more in building trust in institutions, which my thesis found to be the strongest driver of support for the EU in Slovakia.

Sources

European Commission (2026). Standard Eurobarometer 105, Spring 2026. Dataset: GESIS ZA9144.
GLOBSEC (2024). GLOBSEC Trends 2024: Slovakia.
Turková et al. (2023). The penetration of Russian disinformation related to the war in Ukraine: Evidence from Poland, the Czech Republic and Slovakia. International Political Science Review.
