Identity politics · Migration

Migration question back on the shelf until further notice

Migration, which served an essential role in driving Euroscepticism in Slovakia, seems to have lost its top spot to a new concern: the war in Ukraine.

Anti-immigration sentiment is deeply rooted in Slovak society. As the 2023 Eurobarometer survey shows, Slovak respondents scored near the top of a four-point scale on a hostility question regarding refugees, migrants, and minority groups. This meant one of the highest readings among all the member states. Since the 2015 refugee crisis, hostility towards migration has been considered enough to drive Central and Eastern European states away from the EU.

However, my thesis research indicates otherwise. Once you account for institutional trust and the EU's policy towards Ukraine, hostility towards migration does not predict anything. In other words, Slovak citizens still care about migration but measurably less so when there is war next door.

Different crisis, different theory

Identity-based theory developed around the 2015 migration crisis argues that citizens who view their identity in exclusionary terms perceive European integration — including freedom of movement and multiculturalism — as a threat to their own culture. Of course, this is a well-known theory that worked extremely well in explaining Euroscepticism in Central and Eastern Europe. Countries like Poland and Hungary used this sentiment to build governing coalitions.

Looking at the 2023 Eurobarometer data, this theory appears to have a limit that no one thought of testing before: a large-scale security crisis on Slovakia's doorstep. After nearly two years of full-scale war in neighbouring Ukraine, immigration anxiety is crowded out by an immediate threat. Slovak voters who worry about war have less room to think about migrants, even when one person feels both.

Where anxiety shifted instead

The survey data behind my thesis offers a clue as to where anxiety went. According to the GLOBSEC 2024 report, 51% of Slovaks blame the West or Ukraine itself for the Russian invasion, compared with 41% of Slovaks who blame Russia. These are alarming numbers for a member state that belongs to both the EU and NATO. The influence of Russian disinformation campaigns is largely the reason. The war did not just crowd out migration as an anxiety but provided a competing narrative of what a real threat is. This narrative works through institutional trust, not through migration framing or identity.

The broader data supports this: age, not identity, was the strongest demographic predictor of Euroscepticism. Older Slovak respondents who were shaped by the post-communist transition and difficult EU and NATO accessions were significantly less supportive of European integration than the younger generation. The dividing line in Slovak public opinion appears institutional and generational, not cultural.

One Slovakia doesn't make summer

The obvious risk here is treating one survey wave as a permanent shift. Migration hostility in Slovakia hasn't disappeared, and there is no guarantee that it won't resurface once the war in Ukraine fades from public attention or ends entirely. Still, the finding is a useful corrective for anyone tempted to apply a single theory of Euroscepticism across every Central and Eastern European member state. Slovakia in late 2023 is a reminder that drivers of public opinion are fluid, not fixed. Drivers are responses to the most immediate threats that people feel. Geopolitical crises can rewrite the hierarchy quickly, in Slovakia and elsewhere.
